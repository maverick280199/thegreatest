## BEAT 1 — Is this even a 138 case?

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act makes a bounced cheque a criminal offence. Not just a
civil dispute. A criminal case.

But three things must be true.

One — the cheque was given for a legally enforceable debt or liability. An unpaid invoice counts.
A gift does not.

Two — you presented it within its validity. A cheque is valid for three months from its date.

Three — it came back unpaid. Insufficient funds is the classic reason, but courts have held that
returns like "payment stopped by drawer" can also attract Section 138.

And here's the part that helps you: under Section 139, the law *presumes* the cheque was issued for a
debt. The other side has to prove it wasn't.

[ON SCREEN: three ticks appearing — "Enforceable debt", "Presented within 3 months", "Returned unpaid". Then a gavel icon: "Sec. 139 — presumption in your favour".]

---

## BEAT 2 — Deadline one: the legal notice, 30 days

The moment your bank hands you that return memo, the clock starts.

You have **30 days** from receiving the bank's information to send a written demand notice to the
person who issued the cheque.

Miss it, and your criminal remedy is gone for that presentation.

What goes in the notice: the cheque number, date, amount, the bank, the reason for return — and a
clear demand that the cheque amount be paid within 15 days.

Send it to the correct address. Keep the postal receipt and the tracking proof. That paper will
matter in court more than you think.

[ON SCREEN: the timeline's first box fills: "NOTICE — within 30 days of return memo". Checklist: cheque no. · date · amount · bank · reason · 15-day demand · proof of dispatch.]

---

## BEAT 3 — Deadline two: give them 15 days

After the notice is received, the drawer gets **15 days** to pay.

This is where people make the most expensive mistake in this whole process. They get angry, they
get impatient, and they file the complaint on day ten.

Don't. The Supreme Court in *Yogendra Pratap Singh v. Savitri Pandey* held that a complaint filed
before those 15 days are over is premature and cannot be taken cognizance of.

Let the 15 days run. Fully.

[ON SCREEN: second box fills: "15 DAYS — let it run in full". Red stamp over day 10: "PREMATURE". Case name lower-third.]

---

## BEAT 4 — Deadline three: file within one month

If they still haven't paid, your cause of action arises on day sixteen.

From that point you have **one month** to file your complaint before the magistrate.

Which court? For a cheque you deposited in your own account, Section 142 says the case goes to the
court where your bank branch is. You don't have to chase them to their city.

If you are late, the court has the power to condone delay for sufficient cause — but don't build
your case on the hope of a favour.

[ON SCREEN: third box fills: "COMPLAINT — within 1 month". Map pin on the payee's bank branch: "Your branch = your court (Sec. 142(2))".]

## BEAT 5 — If a company gave you the cheque

Very common in business. The cheque is signed by a director, on a company account.

Section 141 lets you proceed against the people in charge of the company's business. But the
Supreme Court in *Aneeta Hada* made it clear — the company itself must be made an accused. Leave the
company out, and you can't proceed against the directors alone.

So: company first, then the directors and signatory responsible.

[ON SCREEN: org chart — COMPANY (accused no. 1) → Director / Signatory. Red cross over a version without the company: "Aneeta Hada (2012)".]

---

## BEAT 6 — What the law can do to them

Why does this work so well for recovery? Because the stakes on the other side are real.

Conviction can mean imprisonment up to two years, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

Under Section 143A the trial court can order interim compensation of up to 20 percent of the cheque
amount while the case is still running. And under Section 148, if they appeal a conviction, the
appellate court can make them deposit at least 20 percent.

And you can file a civil recovery suit alongside — the criminal case doesn't stop you.

[ON SCREEN: three cards — "Up to 2 yrs / 2× fine", "143A: interim up to 20%", "148: appeal deposit ≥ 20%". Fourth card slides in: "Civil suit can run in parallel".]
